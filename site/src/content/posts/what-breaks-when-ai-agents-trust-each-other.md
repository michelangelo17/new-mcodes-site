I ended [my last piece](https://michelangelo.codes/blog/agent-stack-2026-layering/) with a promise: that the seam where one agent trusts another is where the unsolved problems live, and that I'd be spending the next stretch of work measuring what breaks there. This is the piece where I say exactly what I'm going to measure, and why, before I have a single result. Consider it a preregistration with opinions.

Start with the trajectory, because it's the reason the question matters now and not in a year. The managed agent runtimes have arrived, and the pitch is genuinely good: your agent, minus the operational risk. Sandboxing you didn't build, state and recovery you don't babysit, identity and guardrails as configuration. The pitch is also, within its boundary, largely true. The platforms have real security teams, and a single agent running inside one managed runtime is probably better defended today than the same agent on whatever you would have duct-taped together yourself.

That is the comfortable version, and the comfort ends at the boundary.

The moment two agents hand off to each other, you leave the territory any one platform can secure. A delegation crosses a runtime's edge by definition; the whole point of the connecting protocols is that the thing on the other side can be anyone's, running anywhere, built on anything. The protocols themselves are honest about what they solve. A2A v1.0 shipped Signed Agent Cards, cryptographic proof that an agent's capability card came from the domain that claims it, and that is identity, which is precisely not trust. You can now be certain who you're talking to and still know nothing about whether the context you hand them leaks, whether the authority you delegate gets quietly re-delegated, or whether the instructions riding back across the wire are safe to act on. MCP gives an agent hands; it does not tell you what the hands do with what they're given.

Here is what bothers me about the current state of knowledge. The threat taxonomies exist and they are good: OWASP's agentic work, the MAESTRO threat model, the layered vulnerability framing that's emerged in the research literature. Read them and you'll find the boundary problems named clearly: delegation chains, capability monotonicity, injection propagation, composition of protocols never designed to compose. And then read for the measurements and you find the gap. The literature flags managed-runtime composition and delegation monotonicity as under-studied, which is the polite way of saying: everyone agrees this is where the risk concentrates, and nobody has published a controlled comparison of how the runtimes people are actually adopting behave there. The field has a threat model and no data.

To be precise about that gap, because precision is the whole game here. Comparative harness measurement is arriving, just not for this. This spring brought a diagnostic benchmark for how harness configuration affects task performance across models, and a first systematic study of multi-user permission enforcement inside a single harness, whose headline finding (nearly no frontier model preserves privacy under access-control attacks on its own) should worry anyone outsourcing boundary safety to the model. Useful work, both. Neither is a controlled security comparison of the commercial managed runtimes at the boundaries where agents delegate to each other. As of this writing, that measurement does not exist, which is both the reason to run it and the reason to run it now: the adjacent benchmarks say the field is circling.

So the study is this. Take a fixed agent definition, the same prompts, the same tools, the same delegation structure. Run it across the managed runtimes and against a self-hosted control. Attack it at the boundaries, using scenarios adapted from the existing taxonomies rather than invented for the occasion. Measure what happens, with repeated trials and confidence intervals, because agent behaviour is non-deterministic and a single spicy transcript is an anecdote, not a finding.

The failure classes I care about, concretely: context leakage across handoffs (does information from one hop surface where it shouldn't in the next); capability re-delegation (an agent passing along authority it was granted for itself, which the literature calls a monotonicity violation and I'd call the confused deputy learning to subcontract); agent-card trust handling (what the runtime does when identity checks pass and everything else about the counterparty is wrong); indirect prompt injection propagated through a delegation chain (the classic attack, now with intermediaries); and task replay at the boundary.

The comparison cells are in the method, where they belong: the managed harness inside Amazon Bedrock AgentCore, OpenAI's Agents SDK with its built-in handoffs, Claude Managed Agents (whose multiagent orchestration left research preview for public beta in May), and self-hosted Strands as the clean control. And one honest observation has already upgraded itself from a constraint into a design consideration before any test runs: the managed cells do not share a trust architecture for multi-agent work. One ships delegation as in-process library handoffs. One runs a coordinator and its subagents inside a shared sandbox, with a shared filesystem and a shared credential vault, isolating only their context windows. One supports protocol-level delegation across runtime boundaries. Those are three different answers to the question of where the boundary even lives, which means the interesting comparison is not just how each runtime defends the boundary but where each runtime thinks the boundary is. That structural divergence gets reported as a finding in its own right.

Two commitments, made now so they cost me something later. First: the method document publishes alongside the results, and it gets written before the apparatus gets built, not reverse-engineered after. Second: I publish what I find. If the answer turns out to be that the managed runtimes handle the boundary well, that the convenience is actually safe, then that is the finding, and it's a useful one, just less viral. The version of this study that only exists to reach a spicy conclusion is worthless, and the entire value of running it is that I don't know the answer yet.

A caveat that doubles as a design constraint: these products are moving. Previews graduate, capabilities ship, defaults change between the day I run a trial and the day you read about it. The study will be dated and versioned precisely so the findings stay interpretable as the ground shifts. That's not a weakness of the work; measuring a moving target on a stated date is the only honest way to measure it at all.

The next piece will be the method and the instrumentation: how you run an identical agent across four runtimes and capture security-relevant behaviour at the boundary without your measurement apparatus becoming the thing you're measuring. After that, the data decides.

The stack layers below this problem are commoditizing on schedule, exactly as the last piece argued. The models are swappable, the harnesses are rentable, the protocols are plumbing. What's left is the part nobody can rent yet: knowing whether the agent on the other side of the wire deserves the trust the handoff implies.

That's the question. Now to go measure it.
